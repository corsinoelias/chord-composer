**Quick answer:** The chords in the key of C major are **C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, and B diminished** — one chord built on each note of the C major scale. The most common C major chord progressions are **I–V–VI–IV** (C–G–Am–F), **I–IV–V–IV** (C–F–G–F), and **II–V–I** (Dm–G–C). Every example below is playable right now.

## The 7 Chords in the Key of C Major

C major is the only major key with no sharps or flats — one chord built on each note of the scale: **C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, and B diminished**.

**Maj – min – min – Maj – Maj – min – dim** — that's the chord-quality formula behind every major key, not just C's.

## The 7 Chords in C Major, as Seventh Chords

Stack one more note — the 7th — on top of each triad and you get the seventh-chord version of the same seven chords. This is the vocabulary jazz and more advanced arranging use instead of plain triads:

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | I | Cmaj7 | C–E–G–B | Major 7th |
| 2 | II | Dm7 | D–F–A–C | Minor 7th |
| 3 | III | Em7 | E–G–B–D | Minor 7th |
| 4 | IV | Fmaj7 | F–A–C–E | Major 7th |
| 5 | V | G7 | G–B–D–F | Dominant 7th |
| 6 | VI | Am7 | A–C–E–G | Minor 7th |
| 7 | VII° | Bm7♭5 | B–D–F–A | Half-diminished |

Notice the one exception: the V chord is a **dominant 7th** (G7), not a major 7th. That's deliberate — the dominant 7th is what creates the pull back to C. Every other chord in the set keeps its plain major-7th or minor-7th quality.

## Popular C Major Chord Progressions

Every progression below is labeled two ways — Roman numerals for music theory, and the plain-number shorthand (1-4-5, 1-5-6-4…) guitarists and producers actually search for.

### I–IV–V–IV (1-4-5-4) — C–F–G–F

The foundation of blues, rock and roll, and folk. Every chord is major, so it never leaves home — it just visits. Landing back on F instead of stopping cold on G keeps the loop rolling instead of ending in mid-air.

## When C Isn't the First Chord

Songs don't always start on C, even when they're in the key of C. This motion is the strongest signal that a progression is about to land on C major:

### IV–V–I (4-5-1) — F–G–C

The classic cadence. F builds lift, G builds tension, C resolves it — the most reliable way to confirm you're in C major by ear.

<ChordEmbed chords="F G C" bpm={90} style="pop_basic" title="IV–V–I cadence resolving to C" />

The II–V–I above (Dm–G–C) is the other common way songs arrive at C major — the Dm softens the approach before G pulls toward home.

## The C Major Scale

The seven chords above all come from one scale. Understanding it explains why those specific chords exist.

### What notes are in the C major scale?

**C–D–E–F–G–A–B** — the seven white keys on a piano, in order. No sharps, no flats. This is also called the C major *natural* scale, since nothing is altered.

### What is the formula of the C major scale?

Every major scale follows the same interval pattern: **Whole–Whole–Half–Whole–Whole–Whole–Half** (W–W–H–W–W–W–H). Starting on C and applying that formula lands you back on the seven natural notes with no adjustment needed.

### Why does C major have no sharps or flats?

Because C is the note where the W–W–H–W–W–W–H pattern falls exactly on the white keys. Start that same formula from any other note and at least one step needs a sharp or flat to keep the pattern intact. That's why C major is usually the first key taught — there's nothing to alter, just the pattern itself.

### How can I practice the C major scale?

Open the [Guitar Scale Chart](/tools/scales/?root=0&scale=Major%20Scale) — C major is preloaded. Hit **Play** to hear it ascending and descending, or look at the fretboard to see exactly where each note falls.

### What does the C major scale sound like?

Bright and fully resolved — it's the reference sound most ears use to judge "in tune" and "at rest," which is exactly why it's the scale played above in the [practice tool](/tools/scales/?root=0&scale=Major%20Scale).
