**Quick answer:** Reggae harmony is built almost entirely from three shapes: the two-chord **I–V vamp** (G–D), the **I–IV–V turnaround** (G–C–D) inherited from ska and rocksteady, and the darker **i–♭VII minor vamp** (Am–G) used in roots and steppers. What makes them sound like reggae isn't the chords themselves — it's the <dfn>skank</dfn>, the clipped upstroke played on beats 2 and 4.

---

Reggae harmony is deceptively simple. Most classic riddims sit on two or three chords, repeated for the entire song — but the feel lives in *where* you place them. Learn these three progressions and the rhythm that drives them, and you can play the harmonic backbone of nearly every record to come out of Kingston.

## What Is the Skank?

The <dfn>skank</dfn> (also called the "bang" or the "chop") is reggae's signature rhythm-guitar stroke: a short, percussive chord stab played on the **offbeats — beats 2 and 4** — while the bass and kick drum own beat 3. You strum, then instantly mute the strings with your fretting hand so the chord barely rings out: "chka… chka…"

| Beat | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Guitar | rest | **SKANK** | rest | **SKANK** |
| Bass / kick | hit | rest | hit | rest |

Barre chords make this easy — you strum down (or up), then simply release finger pressure on the fretting hand to choke the strings dead. Open chords work too, but they're harder to mute cleanly. Traditionally the stroke is an upstroke hitting the top three or four strings, which gives it a brighter, thinner attack than a full downstroke chord.

Try programming the skank yourself in the [strumming pattern editor](/tools/guitar-strumming-patterns/) — mute the 1 and 3 slots, put an upstroke on 2 and 4, and loop it against the beat.

## 1. The Two-Chord Vamp: I–V

The backbone of roots reggae. Two chords, one bar each, looped for the entire song. In G major that's **G → D**. The magic is in the bounce between tension (V) and release (I) — your ear never gets tired of it, especially once the skank is locked in.

## 2. The Classic Turnaround: I–IV–V

Ska and rocksteady inherited this straight from R&B. In reggae it slows down and stretches out — often two bars on the I chord before moving. Try **G → C → D** and let the bass walk between changes instead of just holding roots.

## 3. The Minor Vamp: i–♭VII

The darker, rootsier sound — think steppers and dub. A minor chord dropping to the flat-seven below it: **Am → G**. Heavy, meditative, and endless. Most militant roots tunes live here, often with almost nothing else happening harmonically for the entire song.

## Reggae Chord Progressions Reference

| Progression | Chords in G | Feel | Where you'll hear it |
|---|---|---|---|
| I–V | G – D | Hypnotic, two-chord vamp | Roots reggae, one drop |
| I–IV–V | G – C – D | Classic turnaround | Ska, rocksteady |
| i–♭VII | Am – G | Dark, meditative | Steppers, dub |

## How to Practice the Skank This Week

1. **Loop one progression at 76 BPM.** Pick I–V, I–IV–V, or the minor vamp above and set a metronome. Ten minutes, no exceptions.
2. **Isolate the choke.** Strum, then release finger pressure immediately. The gap between the "chk" and silence is what separates a skank from a regular strum.
3. **Land exactly on 2 and 4.** Count out loud while you play — "1 (chk) 3 (chk)" — until the offbeat placement feels automatic rather than counted.
4. **Leave space on beat 1 and 3.** The bass and kick own those beats. Resist the urge to fill in — reggae lives in restraint.
5. **Switch progressions without stopping the pulse.** Once each one feels solid on its own, practice moving between the I–V and I–IV–V without breaking the skank pattern.

Once the choke and the offbeat placement feel automatic, try building the [bassline that sits underneath these progressions](/tools/bass-guitar-tab/) — the one drop bass and the skank guitar are two halves of the same rhythm. For a broader look at how these progressions fit into general harmony, see [what a chord progression actually is](/learn/what-is-a-chord-progression/) and [more beginner-friendly progressions](/learn/chord-progressions-for-beginners/) to build from.
