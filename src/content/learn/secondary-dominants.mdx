Most chord progressions stay inside one key. You pick a key — say, C major — and use its seven diatonic chords: C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, and Bdim. That's the foundation of most pop, rock, and folk music, and it works beautifully.

But at some point, you'll notice that certain songs drop in a chord that feels *different* — brighter, more intense, almost like it came from another key. Then the next chord arrives and everything feels resolved. That moment of tension and release is often a <dfn>**secondary dominant**</dfn> at work.

Secondary dominants are the most practical way to add harmonic color without fully changing key. Once you understand them, you'll start hearing them everywhere.

---

## What Is a Secondary Dominant?

A <dfn>**secondary dominant**</dfn> is a dominant seventh chord that resolves to a chord other than the home tonic. In other words: instead of the V chord resolving to I (the standard move), you borrow a temporary dominant chord that resolves to *any other diatonic chord* — treating that chord as if it were the tonic for just a moment.

This is called **tonicization** — briefly making a non-tonic chord feel like a local home.

The shorthand notation uses a slash: **V/X** means "the V chord of chord X." So V/V means the dominant of the dominant. V/ii means the dominant of the ii chord.

Here's the key insight: a dominant seventh chord works as a secondary dominant for *any major or minor chord* in the key. The dominant relationship (V→I) is the strongest resolution in Western harmony, and you can aim it at any target.

---

## Why the V Chord Is So Powerful

Before going further, it helps to understand why the V chord is so powerful in the first place.

The **V chord** (in C major: G or G7) creates tension through a <dfn>**tritone interval**</dfn> — the distance between its third (B) and its seventh (F). That interval spans exactly three whole tones, and it's inherently unstable. The B wants to resolve upward to C, and the F wants to resolve downward to E. Both resolutions happen simultaneously when G7 moves to C major. That's a natural, satisfying cadence.

Secondary dominants borrow that same resolution mechanism and point it at other chords. The result: a chord that creates targeted tension toward a specific destination.

## The Four Most Common Secondary Dominants

In the key of C major, these are the secondary dominants you'll use most often:

| Target chord | Target Roman numeral | Secondary dominant | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| G major | V | **D7** (V/V) | The most common |
| A minor | vi | **E7** (V/vi) | Very common in pop |
| D minor | ii | **A7** (V/ii) | Jazz and gospel staple |
| F major | IV | **C7** (V/IV) | Blues-influenced |

Notice that each secondary dominant is a **dominant seventh chord** — a major triad with a minor seventh added. And each is built a perfect fifth above its target chord.

---

## V/V: The Most Common Secondary Dominant

The most common secondary dominant is **V/V** — the dominant of the dominant.

In C major, the V chord is G. The V chord of G major is **D major** — or more specifically, **D7** when you add the seventh. D7 contains the notes D, F#, A, and C. The F# doesn't belong to C major (which has F natural), but it's a note from G major's scale. That borrowed sharp creates a bright, expectant sound that makes the arrival of G feel especially satisfying.

You hear V/V constantly in popular music. The Beatles' "Let It Be," James Taylor's "Fire and Rain," and hundreds of gospel and country songs use D7 to add lift before hitting the G chord. It's one of those moves that sounds surprising the first time you encounter it but instantly feels *right*.

---

## V/vi: The Secondary Dominant to the Relative Minor

**V/vi** targets the vi chord — the relative minor. In C major, the vi chord is Am. The V chord of A minor is **E major** (or E7). E7 contains G#, which doesn't belong to C major (which has G natural). That raised third creates a brighter, more expectant quality than a plain Em chord.

<ChordEmbed chords="C G E7 Am" bpm={88} style="pop_basic" title="V/vi — E7 tonicizes Am before resolving" />

This move is everywhere in pop and rock. Every time you hear a major chord where you'd expect a minor one (or an unusually bright-sounding chord before an Am), there's a good chance it's V/vi. It makes the Am feel more like a temporary home — more serious, more emotional — than a plain diatonic progression would.

---

## V/ii: The Secondary Dominant to the ii Chord

**V/ii** targets the ii chord — in C major, that's Dm. The V chord of D minor is **A major** (or A7). A7 contains C#, which doesn't belong to C major (which has C natural). That raised third creates a colorful, jazz-inflected sound.

<ChordEmbed chords="C A7 Dm G7" bpm={92} style="pop_basic" title="V/ii — A7 tonicizes Dm" />

V/ii shows up constantly in jazz and gospel. The ii chord has a particular harmonic weight — it's the first step in the ii–V–I cadence — and approaching it with its own dominant makes that weight even heavier. In pop contexts, A7 before Dm gives a slightly flamenco or neo-soul quality.

---

## V/IV: The Bluesy Secondary Dominant

**V/IV** targets the IV chord — in C major, that's F. The V chord of F major is **C major** — but with an added flatted seventh, it becomes **C7**. C7 contains Bb, which doesn't belong to C major (which has B natural). That flat seventh gives C7 a bluesy, slightly unresolved quality.

<ChordEmbed chords="C7 F C G" bpm={88} style="pop_basic" title="V/IV — C7 as a secondary dominant to F" />

V/IV is the most common secondary dominant in the blues. Moving from I7 (C7) to IV (F) is the signature move of a 12-bar blues — the I chord becomes its own secondary dominant, which is why blues music has that inherent tension even on the "home" chord.

## How to Find Any Secondary Dominant

The formula is the same for every target chord:

1. Identify the **root** of your target chord
2. Go up a **perfect fifth** from that root (or equivalently, down a perfect fourth)
3. Build a **dominant seventh chord** on that note (major triad + minor seventh)

Examples in C major:

- Target: **Am** → up a fifth from A = E → build **E7** (E, G#, B, D)
- Target: **Dm** → up a fifth from D = A → build **A7** (A, C#, E, G)
- Target: **G** → up a fifth from G = D → build **D7** (D, F#, A, C)
- Target: **Em** → up a fifth from E = B → build **B7** (B, D#, F#, A)

Notice that each secondary dominant introduces at least one note outside the key. That's the source of the color — the non-diatonic note creates temporary tension that resolves as the target chord arrives.

---

## Secondary Dominants vs. Borrowed Chords

These two techniques often get confused. Here's the distinction:

A **secondary dominant** is a dominant seventh chord that *resolves to a diatonic chord* in the key. The borrowed note is heard as a transient passing tone — it creates tension and immediately resolves. The key center doesn't change.

A **borrowed chord** (also called a modal mixture chord) is a chord taken wholesale from the parallel minor (or another mode) that *doesn't necessarily resolve like a dominant*. For example, a bVII chord (Bb major in C major) borrowed from C Dorian or C mixolydian.

The practical test: if the chromatic chord creates a dominant-to-tonic feeling toward another chord, it's a secondary dominant. If it's used for color without that specific V→I tension, it's probably a borrowed chord.

## Using Secondary Dominants in Your Songwriting

Secondary dominants work best when they're **set up and resolved**. Here are three reliable patterns:

### Pattern 1: The anticipation move
Place a secondary dominant *one beat or one bar before the target chord*. The most common version: V/V one bar before the V chord.

`C → D7 → G → C` (the D7 makes the arrival of G feel more earned)

### Pattern 2: The chain of dominants
Resolve one secondary dominant to a chord that's itself a secondary dominant, creating a descending chain. This is extremely common in jazz and gospel:

`A7 → D7 → G7 → C` (each chord resolves a fifth down, pulling toward I)

<ChordEmbed chords="A7 D7 G7 Cmaj7" bpm={100} style="pop_basic" title="Dominant chain — each chord is a secondary dominant of the next" />

### Pattern 3: Minor-key tonicization
In a minor key, V/iv makes the iv chord feel heavier. In A minor:

`Am → E7 → Am → D7 → G7 → Am`

The E7 reinforces the Am feel; the D7 then creates tension that resolves through G7 back to Am.

## Quick Reference: Secondary Dominants in C Major

| Secondary dominant | Notes | Typical target | Common in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D7** (V/V) | D F# A C | G major | Pop, country, gospel |
| **E7** (V/vi) | E G# B D | A minor | Pop, rock, ballads |
| **A7** (V/ii) | A C# E G | D minor | Jazz, gospel, neo-soul |
| **C7** (V/IV) | C E G Bb | F major | Blues, R&B, jazz |
| **B7** (V/iii) | B D# F# A | E minor | Jazz, folk |
| **F#7** (V/vii°) | F# A# C# E | B dim | Rare, unstable |

V/iii and V/vii° are technically valid but rarely used in pop and rock — the iii and vii° chords don't have enough weight to make the secondary dominant feel satisfying. Stick to V/V, V/vi, V/ii, and V/IV for most situations.

---

## The Takeaway

Secondary dominants are the most direct path from "my progressions sound predictable" to "my progressions have color and direction." They borrow the strongest harmonic move in Western music — the dominant-to-tonic resolution — and aim it at any chord in your key.

Start with V/V (add D7 before your G chord in C major). Once that feels natural, try V/vi (add E7 before your Am). From there, the rest follow logically — they're all the same mechanism applied to different targets.
